Zakat on stocks, ETFs, and index funds

How much zakat you owe on shares depends on why you hold them. Long-term investors pay on the company’s zakatable assets; active traders pay on the full market value.

Short answer: for stocks and funds you hold long-term, the Fiqh Council of North America uses a zakatable-asset percentage of 30% by default. Zakat is 2.5% of that portion: on $10,000 of index funds, about $75.

Long-term (passive) investments

Owning a share means owning part of a business. Zakat on a business is due on its zakatable assets — cash, receivables, and inventory — not on fixed assets like buildings or equipment. A long-term shareholder can’t easily look these up for every company in a fund, so FCNA uses a proxy: 30% of market value is treated as zakatable.

Zakatable value = market value × zakatable percentage

Example

A $58,200 brokerage account in index funds × 30% = $17,460 zakatable. Zakat due at 2.5% is $436.50, if your total wealth meets the nisab.

Active trading

Shares bought to sell for a short-term profit are trade goods, so the full market value is zakatable: $10,000 of trading stock means $250 of zakat.

Zakatable percentage by ticker

Instead of the flat 30%, you can use a fund’s actual ratio. US Zakat Calculator estimates it for each company as current assets ÷ market capitalization, from the latest balance sheet. For ETFs it looks through to the holdings: the weighted average of each holding’s percentage, plus the fund’s cash. Bond and precious-metal funds count at 100%.
