Zakat on 401(k), IRA, and HSA accounts

Scholars agree that retirement savings can be zakatable, but differ on how to count money you can’t fully access yet. Here are the three methods published by North American scholarly bodies, and what each one means for your zakat.

Short answer: under the Fiqh Council of North America’s recommended long-term method, zakat on a 401(k) or IRA held in stock funds is 2.5% of the zakatable portion of the balance (about 30% for stocks), with no deduction for taxes or penalties. On a $100,000 401(k), that is about $750.

The three methods

FCNA Long-term (recommended by FCNA)

Treats the account as a long-term investment. Apply the zakatable-asset percentage to stocks and stock funds; cash, bonds, metals, and Bitcoin count at full value. Taxes and early withdrawal penalties are not deducted, since you won’t pay them unless you withdraw early.

FCNA Short-term

Treats the account as a liquid asset you could cash out today. Start from the full market value, then deduct the income tax and early withdrawal penalty you would owe if you withdrew everything now.

FCNA asks you to choose based on your intent for the funds, and not to mix the two methods.

AMJA Accessible Amount

The Assembly of Muslim Jurists of America holds that full ownership is a condition of zakat, so zakat is due only on what you could withdraw today. Apply the zakatable-asset percentage to stocks, then deduct taxes and early withdrawal penalties from the result.

Worked example: a $100,000 Traditional 401(k)

Assume the 401(k) is invested in stock index funds, with a 30% zakatable-asset percentage, a 22% income tax rate, and a 10% early withdrawal penalty.

MethodCalculationZakat due
FCNA Long-term $100,000 × 30% = $30,000 zakatable $750
FCNA Short-term $100,000 − 22% tax − 10% penalty = $68,000 zakatable $1,700
AMJA $100,000 × 30% = $30,000, − 22% tax − 10% penalty = $20,400 zakatable $510

Zakat due is 2.5% of the zakatable amount, if your total wealth meets the nisab.

Traditional vs Roth 401(k) and IRA

Traditional contributions are pre-tax: withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and withdrawing before age 59½ adds a 10% penalty. Roth contributions are after-tax: qualified withdrawals are not taxed, so only the early withdrawal penalty applies.

This only matters for methods that deduct taxes and penalties (FCNA Short-term and AMJA). Under FCNA Long-term nothing is deducted, so a Roth and a Traditional account with the same balance and investments owe the same zakat.

Age 59½ and HSAs

  • If you are 59½ or older, the 10% early withdrawal penalty no longer applies, so there is no penalty to deduct.
  • HSAs carry a 20% penalty on non-medical withdrawals, which is waived at 65. Neither FCNA nor AMJA addresses HSAs specifically; US Zakat Calculator treats them like other retirement accounts.

Sources

  • Fiqh Council of North America: Zakat on Retirement Accounts (Council-approved, updated Feb 2026) and Zakat on Stocks.
  • AMJA: 16th Imams’ Conference recommendations (2019; AMJA notes these are not its final resolutions), Fatwa 87102 on IRAs (Dr. Hatem al-Haj, 2012), and Fatwa 23284 on the 401(k) (Dr. Main Al-Qudah, 2008).
