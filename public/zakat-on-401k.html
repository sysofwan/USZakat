Zakat on 401(k), IRA, and HSA accounts

Scholars agree that retirement savings can be zakatable, but differ on how to count money you can’t fully access yet. Here are the three methods published by North American scholarly bodies, and what each one means for your zakat.

Short answer: under the Fiqh Council of North America’s default long-term method, zakat on a 401(k) or IRA held in stock funds is 2.5% of the zakatable portion of the balance, with no deduction for taxes or penalties.1 Using FCNA’s 30% estimate for stocks,2 zakat on a $100,000 401(k) is about $750.

The three methods

FCNA Long-term (FCNA’s default)

Treats the account as a long-term investment. Apply the zakatable-asset percentage to stocks and stock funds; cash, bonds, metals, and Bitcoin count at full value. Taxes and early withdrawal penalties are not deducted, because they will not be incurred. FCNA notes that “as a default, most people would find Method 1 more relevant.”1

FCNA Short-term

Treats the account as a liquid asset you could cash out today. Start from the full market value, then deduct the income tax and early withdrawal penalty you would owe if you withdrew everything now.1

FCNA asks you to choose the method that matches your intent for the funds, and not to mix the two.1

AMJA Accessible Amount

The Assembly of Muslim Jurists of America’s imams’ conference holds that complete ownership is a condition of zakat, so zakat is due on retirement funds after deducting taxes and penalties.3 It also asks investors to estimate the zakatable share of their stocks where possible.3 In practice: apply the zakatable-asset percentage to stocks, then deduct taxes and early withdrawal penalties from the result. Earlier AMJA fatwas on IRAs and 401(k)s take a similar approach.4,5

Worked example: a $100,000 Traditional 401(k)

Assume the 401(k) is invested in stock index funds, with a 30% zakatable-asset percentage, a 22% income tax rate, and a 10% early withdrawal penalty.

MethodCalculationZakat due
FCNA Long-term $100,000 × 30% = $30,000 zakatable $750
FCNA Short-term $100,000 − 22% tax − 10% penalty = $68,000 zakatable $1,700
AMJA $100,000 × 30% = $30,000, − 22% tax − 10% penalty = $20,400 zakatable $510

Zakat due is 2.5% of the zakatable amount, if your total wealth meets the nisab. FCNA’s own examples use each fund’s actual zakatable share instead of 30% — for instance 24.8% for VOO and 8.24% for SPUS.1 See zakat on stocks to look up a fund.

Traditional vs Roth 401(k) and IRA

Traditional contributions are pre-tax: withdrawals are taxed as income. Roth contributions are after-tax, and qualified withdrawals are not taxed.7 Withdrawing before age 59½ generally adds a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies.6

This only matters for methods that deduct taxes and penalties (FCNA Short-term and AMJA). Under FCNA Long-term nothing is deducted, so a Roth and a Traditional account with the same balance and investments owe the same zakat.

Age 59½ and HSAs

  • If you are 59½ or older, the 10% early withdrawal penalty no longer applies,6 so there is no penalty to deduct.
  • HSAs carry an additional 20% tax on withdrawals not used for medical expenses, which no longer applies once you reach 65.8 Neither FCNA nor AMJA addresses HSAs specifically; US Zakat Calculator treats them like other retirement accounts.
